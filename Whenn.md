# Whenn

## The core hook

> World clocks tell you what time it is now. Whenn tells you when.

That's the whole pitch in one line — your product name does most of the work, so lean into it.

## One-line tagline options

- World clocks tell you now. Whenn tells you when.
- The world clock for people who actually have to schedule things.
- Pick a time. See it everywhere. Done.
- Stop doing time zone math at 11pm.

## Landing page / short description

> Every world clock answers the same question: what time is it right now? But that's almost never what you actually need.
>
> When you're booking a call with someone in London, Tokyo, or São Paulo, you need to know what time it'll be *there* next Tuesday at 3pm — not what time it is this exact second.
>
> Whenn is built for that one job. Set a time in your location and see where it lands everywhere else. Or flip it — set a time in theirs and see when you'd need to be ready. No mental math. No "wait, is that their tomorrow or yours?" Just the answer.

## Social / launch post (short, personal)

> I kept sending wrong meeting times to people overseas. Every world clock shows you now, but I needed to know *when* — what 3pm Tuesday my time looks like in five other cities.
>
> So I built Whenn. Pick a time anywhere, see it everywhere. That's it.
>
> Live at whenn.pro — would love your feedback on what's missing.

## Feedback ask

> Whenn is early. If you live across time zones and have a strong opinion about what a scheduling tool should do, try it and tell me what's broken. I read everything.

## Writing notes

- The killer insight is the contrast: *traditional clocks tell you now, Whenn tells you future times.* Lead with that — it's the entire reason to care.
- Avoid generic framing like "online meetings with people around the world." The people who feel this pain *viscerally* are remote workers, founders with overseas clients, and freelancers.
- Name the specific micro-trauma: sending the wrong time and getting a 4am "are we still on?" message. That lands harder than the abstract use case.
